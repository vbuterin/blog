Contra Scott on SURA

Epistemic status: medium confidence in all of this. And I think anyone who has more than medium confidence regarding anything about where we're going is totally crazy.

AI is progressing rapidly. In 2024, we were dealing with chatbots. In 2026, we are proving the Riemann Hypothesis. There are roughly two schools of thought about what is going to happen next.

Some believe that AI will keep improving, but it won't (yet) replace everything that humans do. It will (for now) "only" be a technological revolution as big as the industrial revolution or the internet - though even that is utterly world-changing, as everyone outside of Europe found out in the 19ᵗʰ century when they quickly came to realize what it means to not be industrialized when your opponents are. If you believe this, a very natural instinct to have is "well surely something so important to prosperity should be shared among all humanity, and not dominated by a small group of people in one country who can selectively decide who in this new world will be a second-class, first-class and business-class citizen?"

Others believe that we might be less than three years away from superintelligent AI being powerful enough to destroy the world. And even if it won't "take over" completely, it will in the very near term bring massive cybersecurity, physical security, political and biosecurity challenges that our societal infrastructure will need much more time to adapt to. If you believe this, a very natural instinct to have is "WAIT, THOSE PEOPLE SINCERELY BELIEVE THAT AND THEY'RE STILL GOING FULL SPEED AHEAD BUILDING THE THING! IS THIS SOME KIND OF DEATH CULT?".

But many people in frontier AI labs and frontier tech circles have a very different view of what we're supposed to be doing.

The tweet on the left is from Suhail, a semi-prominent US tech entrepreneur.

The note on the right is from Scott Alexander's recent post "Against SURA".

SURA ("Stop Unilaterally, Raise Awareness") is the idea that if you're in a frontier AI company and you believe they're recklessly building potentially world-ending technology, the right thing to do is (1) immediately stop working there, (2) explain your concerns to the world's media and governments.

Scott opposes SURA. Instead, Scott wants to preserve space for "plan C": the good AI companies accelerate as fast as they can, build the really super powerful and dangerous AI with as much lead time as possible before everyone else, then work on improving its safety, and "release [it] at the last possible moment before the racing non-alignment-pilled AI companies beat them".

The two plans have a very different tone - the one on the left openly domineering and jingoistic, and the one on the right wise, carefully-considered and trying hard to do the right thing in the face of very unfavorable incentive structures. But functionally, the two plans amount to basically the exact same thing. Powerful AI is dangerous, we are the good guys, and so we should accelerate to build powerful AI so that we have more space to do the needed good things before the bad guys get to the level of capability where they can be truly dangerous.

As you might have gleaned from the tone of my writing so far, I feel very uncomfortable about both of these plans. And yet, Scott Alexander, the intellectual I have looked up to and considered one of my top-five heroes ever since I was a teenager, is on the other side of this debate from me. In this post, I will try to explain why I disagree.

My argument will be in six parts:

  1. Scott had beautiful high-minded ideas on morality in the 2010s, ideas that deeply imprinted themselves on me and became a cornerstone of my own philosophy.
  2. "Plan C" is the exact opposite of those ideas.
  3. What I think is going on is that the Bay Area AI intellectual scene has shifted from being "large-coalition" to being "small-coalition", and this is upstream of both the crude and the refined versions of the shifts toward accelerationist jingoism.
  4. There are individual weak points in the "against SURA" post. They are non-central to the argument, but they all seem to arise from this "small-coalition" mentality.
  5. Why I have sympathy for open-weights labs in China and elsewhere, and have a hard time taking seriously the pejorative of calling them "distillation attackers".
  6. How would I do AI safety if I were more "ASI-pilled" and more "anti-open-weights pilled" but retained my Scottian metaethics?

Guided by the beauty of our weapons

One of the most important recurring themes in Scott's posts on ethics and culture war in the 2010s was a simple moral insight, echoing and building on the ancient Golden Rule and Kant's Categorial Imperative: treat other people in ways that would make your life and the world better if everyone treated each other in that way.

Scott has made many excellent and widely-celebrated posts in this direction. Two of my favorites are "In Favor of Niceness, Community and Civilization" and "I Can Tolerate Anything Except The Outgroup". Here, I find it fitting to call to attention a slightly less-well-known one: "Guided by the Beauty of our Weapons". In this post, Scott pleads for people to engage in constructive discourse and debate instead of using more aggressive low-brow tactics to get what they want, even if they feel strongly about it.

Quoting Scott:

Logical debate has one advantage over narrative, rhetoric, and violence: it's an asymmetric weapon. That is, it's a weapon which is stronger in the hands of the good guys than in the hands of the bad guys. In ideal conditions ... the good guys will be able to present stronger evidence, cite more experts, and invoke more compelling moral principles. The whole point of logic is that, when done right, it can only prove things that are true.

Violence is a symmetric weapon; the bad guys' punches hit just as hard as the good guys' do ... and the same is true of rhetoric. Martin Luther King was able to make persuasive emotional appeals for good things. But Hitler was able to make persuasive emotional appeals for bad things. I've previously argued that Mohammed counts as the most successful persuader of all time.

Unless you use asymmetric weapons, the best you can hope for is to win by coincidence.

From a zoomed-in point of view, if you're convinced that you're right, you may feel justified in using underhanded tactics to defeat your opponents. But zoomed out from the point of view of wider society, a world where everyone decides that underhanded tactics are okay is much worse than a world where they do not.

There is a first level of morality, one which we are taught as kids, where "evil" consists of valuing selfish goals at the expense of other people's well being, and "good" is the opposite. But this perspective misses a second level of morality that matters just as much. Here, I could quote Scott yet again talking about the holy wars between Catholics and Protestants. But instead, I'll spice things up, and bring in this Chinese podcast talking about the Bitcoin block size war. Here's a translation of part of its introduction:

... you make a bet on something that you believe to be true, and then wait for the world to catch up to your judgement.

But these videos do not answer a more difficult question. What happens if there are two equally great ideals, both with loyal believers, bet everything, and then they collide, where one must die for the other to survive?

In human history, we see many situations like this: on a battlefield, both sides believe they are just, and this belief is not just with words but comes with a real cost. ... This is the deepest structure of tragedy. Not right defeating wrong, but two things that are both right colliding, and one must die for the other to survive, or they both perish.

What if this is not a rare exception, but rather what the average human conflict looks like? What if in the average situation where you think you need to win because you know you are right, actually you are attacking something else which has equally valid cause to know that it's right, and if you show your opponent no mercy, then you are leading the world to ruin?

In such a world, seeking to overly impose your own conception of justice in a way that creates destructive conflict with, or tramples upon, the conceptions of others starts looking indistinguishable from selfishness.

Scott's great moral triumph of the 2010s was steadfastly and autistically sticking to exactly these beliefs, throughout the highs and the lows of both "woke" and "anti-woke". Are you a soldier of social justice fighting against the abhorrent evils of racism and sexism in our culture? Then you get a turn to speak - but not the right to exclude other perspectives. Do you believe that America needs socialism, or a king? Then too, Scott will give your ideas a fair chance - but then join the field of debate himself as a contestant, and smack your ideas down in fair play with his own bare hands. Thus were his lived ideals in debate - and in every other human endeavor.

This is the Scott Alexander that I admire. And my problem with "against SURA" is that I believe that "plan C" is the exact opposite of all of these ideals.

Plan C is not a beautiful weapon

Let us recap again what "Plan C" is. Here is my summary:

  1. Today, "alignment-pilled AI companies" race, and widen their lead over non-alignment-pilled AI companies as much as possible.
  2. When we're right at the critical point where AI is about to be superintelligent in a really dangerous way, alignment-pilled AI companies (possibly there's just one) coordinate a pause among themselves.
  3. Alignment-pilled AI companies switch their full focus to making one AI as safe (ie. aligned with human values) as possible.
  4. At the last possible moment before non-alignment-pilled AI companies catch up, they release their aligned AI into the world.

From their internal perspective, this plan makes perfect sense. As far as I can tell, both from reading Scott's post and from private conversations from people tangentially involved in this whole situation, there are two main beliefs that push toward this conclusion:

  • Alignment is not that hard. It's hard enough that if you're sloppy (ie. not alignment-pilled) you'll fail and screw up, but it's easy enough that an alignment-pilled company can totally manage it.
  • The bigger risk is less misalignment, more misuse - either malicious people or malicious governments using powerful AI to launch cyberattacks, political manipulation attacks, super-viruses or slaughterbots.

On the alignment issue, Scott's model looks something like this:

AIs inherit "virtue" from pretraining, where they generally want to imitate humans and follow their persona-trained helpful assistant character. They are "corrupted" by RLVR, which teaches them power-seeking.

[We can tweak] various parameters and reward schemes to minimize the corrupting effect of RLVR as long as possible, and keep AIs broadly within the virtuous pretraining-dominated regime. If we delay [the corrupting effect of RLVR] past the point where [we + AI] are smart enough to solve alignment properly, then we win.

Again, this is a common view. What I repeatedly hear from private conversations is that lots of people inside or closely connected to Anthropic think that alignment is not that hard.

And on the misuse issue, this recent Anthropic paper is a good place to start.

Needless to say, there are a lot of "big ifs" in here. Here are a few ways in which this plan could go wrong:

  • AI alignment could turn out to be much harder to solve than they think, and AIs will behave deceptively to look more aligned than they are.
  • AI progress could turn out to be much slower than they think. The most critical moments may happen far enough in the future that "alignment-pilled" people may no longer be in charge, either because they get replaced within their companies, or because they themselves change - remember, values drift among effective altruists is around 10% per year!
  • Their model of misuse risk includes random individuals, and it includes anti-US governments, but it does not include the US itself (though the paper does mention one "Israeli-Singaporean commercial intelligence vendor"). It definitely does not (properly) include the company itself. This could end up being a fatally wrong threat assessment.
  • Their AI alignment strategy could turn out to be a dead end, and racing could take away the time that outsiders need to come up with better plans.
  • Their plan of what to tell the aligned AI to do once it has control over the world could turn out to be wrong, and racing could take away the time that outsiders need to come up with better plans.

Fundamentally, plan C a fragile plan. Now, let's see how it compares against Scott's key criterion: is plan C a beautiful weapon?

The most important property for a beautiful weapon to have is: if both you and your opponent use it, then the world becomes better.

What happens if both Anthropic and one of their rivals use Plan C? To simply declare "well obviously, they would fight each other" would be too easy. After all, the plan says that alignment-pilled AI companies would cooperate. So if two companies both agree on Plan C, then the theory goes, they will notice that each other are alignment-pilled, they will race now, coordinate on a stop later, and then help each other on alignment.

But will that actually happen? Oliver Habryka in his response in the comments seems to be quite pessimistic:

If it was two copies of Anthropic, but with different beliefs about how those 6-12 months of lead should be burned, they would both race, as far as I can tell

And from my private conversations, my impression is that Anthropic and DeepMind already, in reality, each consider themselves to be alignment-pilled and the other to be insufficiently careful.

And so ... oops, under Plan C, two slightly different perspectives on the technicals of morality (and probably different human character judgements of each other) already look indistinguishable from two selfish actors fighting to dominate.

We should also remember that more broadly, alignment-pilled AI companies operating under the logic "we are trustworthy and careful, you are not" have a long history of accidentally creating exactly what they fear. The history of "alignment-pilled AI companies" so far includes:

  • Founding OpenAI, because they thought that Google and other major Web2 companies could not be trusted to build powerful AI responsibly.
  • Founding Anthropic, because they thought that OpenAI could not be trusted to build powerful AI responsibly.
  • Founding SSI ("Safe Superintelligence"), because they thought that OpenAI and Anthropic could not be trusted to build powerful AI responsibly.

And so, oops, now there's a race. And then it goes further. Recently, Anthropic greatly weakened their responsible scaling policy, arguing that it's not sustainable in a competitive environment. The problem is: by several accounts, the competitive environment of 2026 is Anthropic's own creation.

Quoting Jacob Coxon, who recently left over safety concerns:

Another account is this WSJ article, which claims that OpenAI's decision to cut back on "side projects" and focus on "coding, enterprise businesses" (ie. things in the hot path of The Race) was directly motivated by Anthropic's success early this year - meaning, Mythos.

Finally, we should not forget that a similar dilemma applies at the level of countries. Anthropic's threat assessment document does not treat US administrations as potential threat actors. And while you might argue that that particular document was directed toward US policy audiences, and of course you don't tell the bear that bears might be scary and bad, that pattern seems to hold across their communications more generally. Here's a quick AI analysis from Claude:

While it is admirable that Anthropic has pledged "no mass surveillance of Americans" and seems to have stuck by it, neither I nor most of my friends nor most of the world are protected by that pledge. Needless to say, as a citizen of the so-called "51ˢᵗ state", I am not satisfied with this threat model - and I think many others feel the same way.

And so an "alignment-pilled" AI company located outside the US, even if it had exactly identical beliefs to either Anthropic or DeepMind, would still likely treat both as outside its coalition and race to get ahead of them, precisely because it would rationally predict that at the critical moment, Anthropic and DeepMind would become controlled not by Anthropic or DeepMind, but by a (to them) hostile or recklessly irresponsible government.

Thus, not just at the level of companies, but also at the level of countries, different "alignment-pilled" people with different opinions and different biases on human character judgement end up looking indistinguishable from selfish power-seekers.

(For completeness, I should also add that even an American AI lab may distrust another AI lab because of its US political exposure. After all, treating one's own government as being part of the threat model is a beautiful quintessential American political value, and one lab may well judge that another is not taking that commandment sufficiently seriously)

In "In Favor of Niceness, Community and Civilization", Scott writes:

Liberalism does not conquer by fire and sword. Liberalism conquers by communities of people who agree to play by the rules, slowly growing until eventually an equilibrium is disturbed. Its battle cry is not "Death to the unbelievers!" but "If you're nice, you can join our cuddle pile!"

Plan C is not like this. Plan C wishes precisely to conquer by fire and sword - and that's the problem.

From large-coalition tech to small-coalition tech

From the first day that I discovered the rationalist world, one of my favorite things about it was that it was inclusive and defined by values (and not eg. language, country of origin or race). If you can find it in your heart to appreciate the earnest quest to become "less wrong", to value intellectual discourse, to value intellectual freedom, and to have a positive-sum worldview, then in principle you can be part of the tribe.

Similarly outside the rationalism bubble, technology as a whole had a spirit of freedom and cosmopolitanism: the whole point is that your race, gender, nation, language and sexual orientation do not matter. We are liberating humanity together.

Even when the zeitgeist was more specific than that in practice, it would center around things like this (quoting Scott):

liberal political beliefs, vague agnosticism, supporting gay rights, thinking guns are barbaric, eating arugula, drinking fancy bottled water, driving Priuses, reading lots of books ... Dawkins-style atheism, vague annoyance that the question of gay rights even comes up, eating paleo, drinking Soylent ... getting conspicuously upset about the War on Drugs and the NSA, and listening to filk

This is a specific culture responding to a specific environment at a specific time (I don't even know what "filk" is), but nevertheless, it is a pattern of behaviors and thoughts and a "vibe" that is in principle open for anyone to adopt.

Now, let's look at the "we" of 2026:

If Plan C is important, then this suggests we ought to be trying to alignment-pill leading companies, or put alignment-pilled companies in the lead - rather than demand that these companies leave the race or burn their lead

Who is "we"? It's a highly specific group of people that's in a position to convince leading AI labs. It's still defined by values, but the value system here is not some plausible human universal like compassion, reason or freedom; rather, it's being "alignment-pilled", a description specific enough that, in context, it clearly excludes even OpenAI. (see also: this post describing coalition shrinking in terms of who is even participating in the discussion)

To show a different example, let's look at Leopold Aschenbrenner, and what he thinks "we" should do given the race with China:

We want to get in a position where it is clear that the United States, a coalition of democratic allies, will win. It is clear to the United States, it is clear to China. That will require having locked down the secrets, having built the 100 gigawatt cluster in the United States, having done the natural gas and doing what's necessary.

When it is clear that the democratic coalition is well ahead, you go to China and offer them a deal ... We're like, "look, we're going to respect you. We're not going to use superintelligence against you. You can do what you want. You're going to get your slice of the galaxy.

We're going to benefit-share with you. We're going to have some compute agreement where there's some ratio of compute that you're allowed to have, enforced with opposing AIs or whatever. We're just not going to do this volatile WMD arms race to the death.

It's a new world order that's US-led, democracy-led, but respects China and lets them do what they want.

Leopold continues further, and also proceeds to claim that "we need to be working on a Stuxnet for the Chinese project". Basically, Leopold Aschenbrenner's vision for how to handle China is that he wants to crush them, and then force them to sign on to a protocol limiting how many boxes they can run.

My overarching point here is: the shift from "large-coalition" to "small-coalition" is not just something pushed by some subgroup that we all agree are awful, xenophobic and terrible selfish people. It's everywhere in frontier AI, even among the respectable people.

The UBI

There is one thing that we can say in the small coalition's defense. Frontier AI labs do have a plan for how to make the fruits of AI available to everyone, a plan that has been echoed by everyone from Sam to Dario to Elon. Usually it's some form of "there should be a UBI" or "everyone should own a share of the AI companies".

But who is that "everyone"?

Let's explore a recent OpenAI paper. It advocates a Public Wealth Fund:

Public Wealth Fund. Create a Public Wealth Fund that provides every citizen—including those not invested in financial markets—with a stake in AI-driven economic growth.

Oh. Every "citizen". That is, every American, leaving 96% of the world's population in the dust.

To make sure I am not simply being captured by my own confirmation bias, I asked AI to do a broader review. Here was its summary:

Geographic scope: All three [frontier AI labs] are primarily or initially USA-framed in their detailed writings (US citizens, US tax base, US policy examples). Broader "all of humanity" or global framing appears mainly in high-level mission statements or abundance rhetoric rather than specific mechanisms.

This is where I predict things will go - and that's the best case, if the coalition does not get even smaller. This is a fundamental bug of the "UBI" meme - and, for that matter, "Universal Basic Compute", and anything where we have some notion of equality delivered through a centralized institution: it distributes access but not power, and the former without the latter is fundamentally unstable.

Now, I fully believe that these are good people with good intentions, and they sincerely believe that of course countries are a terrible basis for determining who should and should not be a second class citizen in the most important technological wave in history, and so actually everyone in the world deserves a share of the UBI. But when it comes time to transfer from the abstract and ideological to the concrete, that idealism falls away. Here is the core problem: there isn't actually any mechanism or pressure for them to follow through on the global UBI. Instead, they face strong pressures from the US political system, and roughly zero pressures from any outside entity (though Carney is admirably trying to "rally the middle powers", which can hopefully form the seed of a viable counterweight)

The coalitions are shrinking

It's not humanity, it's "democracies". It's not democracies, it's America. It's not America, it's "alignment-pilled" people in the Bay Area. And it's not the Western Alignment-Pilled Bay Area Altruist Council of 1879, it's the Western Alignment-Pilled Bay Area Altruist Council of 1912. I worry that there has been a deep shift in subconscious outlook, among rationalists (like Scott), tech entrepreneurs (like Suhail) and many others, going from a 2010s-era large-coalition mentality to something that you could logically argue is still large-coalition and united-by-values, but is in practice a far more narrow thing, captured (ok fine, shaped) by the social and political idiosyncrasies of being a specific cluster of people that is physically concentrated in the San Francisco Bay Area.

To be clear, I am not saying that all human actions should be organized with the interests of a large part of humanity as their end goal. Focusing mostly on yourself and your small tribe is fine. That's why I remain so positive toward "startup society"-like things! But even that is in fact a large-coalition strategy: your own actions are "my people focus on themselves", but the meta-game you are supporting is "everyone's people are free to focus on themselves", which is large-coalition. What is not fine is only listening to a small tribe, while taking actions that overturn the whole world.

We have seen this failure mode before - in exactly the place where Scott has done such an excellent job resisting it: the shift from social liberalism to "woke":

  • First, we had a large-coalition ideology of democracy, some balance between economic liberty and equality, and free speech - sure, you could disagree with parts of it, but it was something that anyone - including my then-teenage dad, illicitly listening to Radio Svoboda from inside the USSR - could feel themselves part of.
  • Then, it became LGBT rights - okay, it's much more specific, and you could reasonably ask "why focus so much on that, and not housing market liberalization and curing malaria?", but it's still fundamentally based on a broad vision of liberating people to be who they want to be.
  • After that, suddenly we were told that free speech is bad, punctuality is part of white supremacy culture, shoplifting and looting are justice, and that we had to follow highly specific directives on the right way to treat transgender people in sports.

The coalition shrunk too much, and that was its undoing. The situation around the "Plan C" school of AI safety may have very different aesthetics because of what kinds of people are involved, but it strikes me as fundamentally similar.

Individual weaknesses in "against SURA" reflect small-coalition mentality

Scott's post contained some individual statements that I pretty directly disagree with. This is rare for me when reading Scott! Let's go through a few disagreements one by one.

Deontology

One of the arguments that Scott argues against at length is the deontological argument (or, if you like, "common sense morality"): isn't it just plain wrong to race full speed ahead building something that you sincerely think might actually destroy the world? Like, we don't allow countries to just kill a hundred thousand innocent civilians and then argue that actually there's some complicated intellectual reason why their actions actually lead to the least-bad feasible outcome given the difficult circumstances ... well ok fine, turns out we're worse at that one than I had hoped. But there's a reason why the better angels of our nature have been trying to create a world where you can't just do that.

Isn't gambling with the lives of literally all of humanity something that you just ... don't have the right to do, and so if you're doing it you should just stop?

Scott says no:

But should there be a deontological prohibition on racing towards superintelligence, regardless of whether it has good consequences?

I'm too uncertain on what determines morality to say anything about it with confidence, but the analogy that comes to mind is consumer use of AI, especially by safety organizations.

If no consumer ever used AI products, then AI companies wouldn't make any money, they would go bankrupt, and we would not get dangerous superintelligence. Therefore, it seems like maybe you and I (and especially organizations working on AI safety) should boycott consumer AI products.

This strikes me as a very weak argument.

The moral difference between racing to build super-dangerous AI, and paying for the products of someone else who is doing that, is structurally the same as the moral difference between buying oil from a country that is invading its neighbors, and joining the invasion yourself. Of course, many of us look negatively at even the former, but the latter is beyond-the-pale in a way that the former never will be.

And it has to be this way. You have to draw lines somewhere. Categorical taboos are much more stable than threshold taboos, so it's better to define the line by category than by some standard of "you can only do things whose harm is less than X". In an interconnected world, every action is at least somewhat contributing to or detracting from everything, and at some point we can only stay sane by drawing a line and tolerating things on one side of the line. And the brightest line you can draw is: helping something indirectly, eg. through market transactions or publicly supporting them on social media, is a mere negative externality, while doing the thing directly is an ethics violation (see also: this excellent Scott Alexander article explaining the difference).

So it may well be that there's some other reason why working at a frontier AI lab is not unethical. But "buying services from someone doing the thing is ok, so doing the thing must also be ok" is not it.

Do we need strong alignment-pilled AI companies to win a "pause AI" fight?

A major section in the "against SURA" post argues that the presence of alignment-pilled AI companies will make it much easier to get a global pause:

But the presence of alignment-pilled companies is one of the strongest levers that we have to change the political situation and enact a global pause. Eliminating them makes it harder to win political fights, convince the masses, and convince China.

II.A: Getting rid of alignment-pilled people and companies makes it harder to win political fights

To me, this feels completely backwards. To me, it feels obvious that if your goal is to create political mood for a global AI pause, one of the worst things you can do is to create an environment where there are strong "alignment-pilled" AI companies that have a plausible claim to being The Good Guys. In their AI work, such companies may well do the right thing. But in terms of their impact on politics, the most important first-order effect will be that a bunch of people will think "why are those crazy luddites trying to pause AI entirely? There are good AI companies, look at how well-intentioned GoodCorp is!"

In fact, if your goal is to create political mood for a global pause, then it seems more plausible that you want is the exact opposite: all the frontier AI companies get their top staff to do terrible things that inspire maximum horror and sensationalist negative public reaction (I will leave it to the reader to imagine what those are), and let themselves get caught.

Is this not obvious? Am I crazy?

(Disclaimer: don't actually do that)

Lower down, Scott argues that one specific good thing that Anthropic does for public discourse and public policy is lobbying for stronger AI regulation. But my impression is that Anthropic is also lobbying against many AI safety bills, as is basically every major AI company. At least from afar, AI lobbying even from the most safety-oriented companies seems to be most forceful when their vision of safety is aligned with their institutional interest - such as going against China or open source - and most muted and self-sabotaging when those two interests diverge.

The whole Frodo's Ring thing

Quoting Scott:

Suppose someone claimed you could trust them with the One Ring. You could trust them because they were only doing it to keep the Ring away from other, worse people, thus solving the problem of there being a Ring at all. Sure, they had a complicated history of sometimes using the Ring's powers when it was, you know, important. But those were, like, emergencies, and they promise not to do it again, except in, you know, more emergencies. Besides, if you don't give it to them, then the worse people will get it, and it's not like you can just leave it lying around or something.

The person who claimed this was named Frodo Baggins, and he saved the world.

This strikes me as very confused about what actually happened in the Lord of the Rings. Namely:

  • Frodo's plan for what to do with the Ring was to destroy it, not to keep it for himself.
  • Frodo's plan was credible, because Frodo was just obviously the type of person who has no megalomania whatsoever. He had already spent seventeen years with the Ring, and basically just thought it was an invisibility gadget and only used it to have fun in the Shire.
  • From the perspective of the "powers that be" at Rivendell, letting Frodo take the Ring was an abdication of power, not a claiming of it. Each of the groups with swords was not comfortable with the other group with swords taking the ring, and so they all coordinated on entrusting it to the only guy who came without a sword (and then gave him a sword just in case).
  • After learning of the Ring's potential (and the Ring itself becoming more "active"), Frodo only had six months to get corrupted by it before it was destroyed in Mount Doom.
  • Even still, ultimately, Frodo failed to avoid being corrupted. The Ring was only destroyed because Gollum arrived and the remaining situation unfolded in a chaotic way that just happened to lead to the right outcome.

Thus, Frodo's tale is very far justifying taking the ring yourself and using it to defeat armies of people trying to produce rival rings.

The commonality that I see in all three cases is a small-coalition mentality: a credulous background attitude toward paths that lead through the ingroup being the heroes that carry the torch of heroic responsibility. Deontology can't say that racing forward with AI and gambling with the lives of all humanity is bad because, well, then the builders can't be heroes. Frontier labs having good reputations must be good for a pause because, well, they have to be heroes. And of course Frodo is a good metaphor for an alignment-pilled AI company because, well, he's a hero.

This is the dark side of an unconstrained ethic of heroism: it can make the inflation of one's own (and one's ingroup's) ego feel like an altruistic act of taking on a burden. (I am sure that I personally have acted counterproductively on many occasions because I had not properly imbibed this lesson)

There is no malice. There is no imperial will to dominate for the sake of dominating. There is a sincere heartfelt desire to carry the burdensome torch of heroism for the benefit of the world. The problem is again: it's a small coalition, and other groups will feel perfectly entitled to make their own coalitions, and so on the whole, this is all not robust to a worldview where the real battle is not good versus evil but, as 脑总described in his podcast, "two equally great ideals, both with loyal believers, betting everything, and then colliding".

How do I feel about the open-weights labs?

There is a strong vibe that you can get from some national-security and frontier-lab circles, that they consider (mostly Chinese) AI labs making open-weights models to be a malicious enemy - the phrase "fraudulent distillation attackers" appears often. I personally have a very hard time sympathizing with this mentality - I feel significantly warmer toward the open-weights labs than I do toward proprietary labs.

But from within the perspective of Plan C, this mentality makes perfect sense. Open-weights distillation labs are a primary threat to Plan C, not just because they are competition and thus shrink how far ahead anyone implementing Plan C is ahead of everyone else, but also because they increase the extent to which it's not possible to uplift your own capabilities without uplifting all of humanity's capabilities in the process - and so they contribute to a dynamic where racing ahead is self-defeating.

I agree with this analysis on a purely positive "what will be the results of which actions" level - except I think it's not "distillation attackers" that need to burn, it's Plan C.

On China

Most of the powerful open-weights labs (which do a lot of distilling, but also do a lot of meaningful technical innovation of their own, particularly in making it easier to run AI on limited hardware) are based in China. But what do people in China actually think about the whole situation?

Recently I visited Shenzhen, and got at least a glimpse of an answer.

You can see why e/acc types like this place.

If you've been following at all, you might have seen the Deepseek engineer comparing the threat of Anthropic winning the AI race to Hitler getting the nuclear bomb before the Allies. But the answer that I heard in person that was most memorable to me was not ideological, mentioning neither abstract principles around openness and fairness, nor Hitler, nor even for that matter the Century of Humiliation. Rather, it was very practical. Roughly, translating from memory:

People are using the frontier American models to cyberattack us. We don't have access to frontier American models. So we need to use powerful open models to defend ourselves.

This echoes what I have seen and heard indirectly. The dominant mood is not high-minded ideals about organizing the galaxies of the universe in the correct manner, or for that matter concern about superintelligence alignment risks. The dominant mood is more: "this is the next technological frontier, and we have to stay afloat".

As an AI might say, the "honest summary": both sides feel insecure. But one side is feeling insecure about being two steps behind, while the other side is feeling insecure about being "only" two steps ahead.

Which side is more deserving of our support? Is it the people who insist that to be safe, they alone must be four steps ahead? Or the people who are trying to keep up, and on top of this are opening up their models to the whole world so that everyone can stay on the same footing?

Standing with the Pirates

At a raw emotional level, this is why I cannot find it in me to oppose "distillation attackers". Even if distillation were all that the open-weights labs did (it's not, they do all kinds of pioneering innovation), that would just mean that are the new Pirates. But the Pirates are the good guys.

Stand with the Pirates.

One moral argument that I could make here is one that many have made before me: pointing out the hypocrisy of the situation. Pretty much all modern AI depends on training data that was obtained via copyright infringement on a massive scale, a topic that has been the subject of multiple recent and ongoing lawsuits. Doing this, and then saying that others are not allowed to pirate off of you, is an act of pulling up the ladder right behind oneself.

But I also want to go back to the moral arguments made by the original Pirates of the 00s.

An important and now mostly forgotten argument that they made centers around the importance of "free culture". If you release a movie, and that movie gets seen by a large chunk of the population, it acquires a kind of network effect lock on our culture. If everyone else has seen it, and you have not, you are not just missing out, you are harmed - you don't understand the metaphors everyone else is now using, you are at least to a slight extent excluded into a second-tier memetic universe. And so people have a moral right for "systemically important" culture to be accessible to them.

(If your response is, "come on don't be so dramatic, pay the $10, it's not much", remember that (i) region locks exist so for some the barrier is not just $10, and (ii) the price being only $10 is itself the result of a cultural settlement which in part resulted from pressure from Pirates)

I claim that the same is happening with AI, and the exact same moral argument applies here too, in a much stronger form.

First, as we saw above with cybersecurity, attackers using powerful AI means that defenders also need powerful AI just to stay where they are.

Second, in the era of AI, anyone not using AI becomes uncompetitive and out of a job. This one has been discussed by many people at length, and so I will leave it here.

Third, there is this interesting subtle point from Jesus Fernandez-Villaverde, linked by Tyler Cowen:

Will AI poison the public finances of many modern states? There is a real risk that it will.

Yesterday, I circulated a new paper, "Saving for the Machines," which makes a simple point. Building the AI transformation will require enormous investment. The only way to induce agents to save for that investment is through a large increase in the real interest rate. For instance, in my benchmark scenario, the ten-year real yield jumps from 5.1% to 13.6% on impact ...

The adopter has a way out. The capital share goes to one, so net capital income is about 88% of GDP, and a flat tax on it can cover the surplus ...

The grim news is that the bystander economy has no way out. Its capital income is still a third of GDP, which grows at 1.6%, and the surplus it needs is 11% of GDP in 2030, 26% in 2035, and 44% forever with unit elasticity (18% and 24% with an elasticity of 0.2). On a balanced primary budget, its debt doubles by 2033 and triples by 2035. No tax system raises that.

Thus, creating such a powerful new technology, and locking some people out of having it, is not a neutral act toward the people you are locking out, leaving them at worst in the same place as if you had never created the new technology at all. It can actively put a huge chunk of the world's population under the boot.

Now, one could criticize open-weights labs for one thing: they are still labs, and they are making AI progress go faster. But my impression is that their contibution to accelerating the frontier is small, and probably outweighed by the decelerationist pressure they create by making it harder to monetize AI, reducing the investment that a proprietary lab can get.

"Yes, but this is a good thing"

(Note that this does not apply to hardware companies (eg. NVIDIA). I see those as a mainly accelerationist force)

The approach proposed by some is that the technology should be tightly controlled, but its fruits should be available to everyone - basically, the "global UBI" plan, or perhaps "universal basic ownership shares". The problem I see with this plan is that it has no political flywheel that naturally enforces it. We already see "UBI for humanity" (the vision that originally helped to create Worldcoin) sliding into "UBI for one country". And it may well slide into something much more restrictive than even that. If we get to a point where it looks like the fate of global power might be decided within months not years and even the next election starts feeling too far away to matter (or if AI super-persuasion makes voters' interests irrelevant by making it possible for powerful people to convince anyone of anything), it quite likely will.

Basically, whereas the usual criticism of open source maximalism is that it's technologically naive ("what? you want to give everyone nukes?"), every presently-existing plan other than open source maximalism (or Pause) strikes me as politically naive - it promises us that there will be broad sharing of the fruits to humanity, but gives no one outside the frontier labs and maybe one country's government any actual leverage to ensure that anyone follows through on that sharing - in fact, the political leverage goes in the other direction.

Is my approach rational given the probabilities and the scales of consequences involved?

At the start of this post, I showed the two main widely-held visions of where AI is going. Either it's the new source of industrial power, and whoever controls it will utterly dominate whoever does not - and so if we don't want a one-world hegemony it must be available to everyone, or it's something much more powerful than even that, and might destroy everyone within three years.

I personally really have no clue which world we are living in. Both sides make strong points. On the one hand, AI literally proved the Riemann hypothesis. On the other hand, every smart person I talk to who is actually working in biology or bio-security tells me that there are many obstacles to creating mirror life or a super-virus, these obstacles cannot be circumvented just by thinking harder, and so even superintelligent AI will not be able to spit those out within twenty years, let alone two.

To the extent that the "AI will become very dangerous very quickly" view has credence, even open-source AI progress is something that we will need to slow down - not because it's open-source, but because it's AI progress.

One viewpoint you might have is: surely, if you give the extreme-AI-danger world any credence at all, the sheer magnitude of the consequences at play should overshadow any concerns within any other scenario? As I've hopefully made clear, in any world where economic and political power of humans continue to be meaningful concepts, I consider Plan C to be utterly ruinous. But surely, once you give the more extreme worlds any credence at all, things like "freedom", "fairness", "who gets to dominate whom?" become mere insignificant monkey-brain concerns relative to the existential risk that all of humanity might get outright eliminated by misaligned or misused AI?

I have two replies to this. First, here is a poll that Scott made in 2014:

The question that Scott asked is approximately: if your current life is worth 1, and death is worth 0, what number is [put condition here] worth? If you weigh blindness at 0.6, that means that you see being blind as being roughly equally bad to a 40% chance of death.

As it turns out, people really do value lots of things other than just being alive. People value comfort, people value social status, people value human connection, and people value dignity and freedom. Being North Korean was ranked 60% as bad as being dead. Even a high-level social and psychological construct like being discriminated against was seen as comparable to an 11% chance of death.

You might argue: extinction is not just about us and our generation, it's about all of human posterity. But guess what - so is total disempowerment (to a human hegemony or a bot).

Hence, I think it's a totally valid viewpoint to say: being free and not being under a hegemony (human or AI or otherwise) can be just as important as a few percentage points of p(doom). This is not an unusual extreme libertarian view. Across thousands of years of human history, people across ages and across cultures have stared at high-level psychological constructs like freedom, dignity and fairness in the face, said "this is important enough that it's worth risking a single-digit (or even double-digit) probability of death to preserve or achieve it", and voluntarily joined an army to prove it.

A second point is that my morality is not pure linear utility-maximization, and I am okay with this. If I look at how I make moral judgements in other contexts, I find that I have a number of "sub-agents" inside me - an inner humanitarian, an inner libertarian, an inner technocrat, an inner human being who values fun, and so on, and I make judgements not by taking a linear combination of these views, but by voting between them, with a large bias toward doing nothing if an issue is close to being split down the middle.

A linear utility-maximizer would say: this approach is irrational. The Condorcet paradox shows how mere voting can turn consistent sub-agents into an inconsistent whole, and you can prove that only linearity escapes this problem. And even a slight amount of inconsistency can lead to an agent getting exploited. If you value 1 apple = 2 oranges = 4 kumquats, this is consistent and not exploitable. But if your values are even slightly dependent on framing - say, you value 10 apples < 19 oranges, 19 oranges < 37 kumquats, 37 kumquats < 9 apples, then I can find three people like you, make each trade with one of you, and on net extract one apple from the three of you, while all three of you think you benefited. In fact, the bias toward doing nothing is a necessary patch that prevents this kind of exploitation from totally ruining us.

This is a kumquat by the way. I somehow grew up only knowing about kumquats as an example fruit in game theory textbooks, so this was new to me too.

But the linear utility-maximization approach has a bigger failure mode: it has basically zero robustness. If you can trick even one of the sub-agents into making a big mistake, you can trick the whole mind.

Hence, I don't think you fundamentally can convince me to accept a plan that most of my mind, with all the principles and values I have been trained with since childhood, finds abhorrent, by appealing to only one part of my mind and telling it that it should override the rest because the consequences are a billion times bigger. It's possible that this mode of thinking is leading me into an error, but this is how my mind operates.

Third, I have a more practical counter-point. Purely in an empirical sense, I think Plan C may fail, because many of the threat actors are outside of its implied threat model. Purely as an empirical matter, I believe that a leading source of p(doom) is highly prestigious, respectable and powerful organizations, that will inevitably be able to get themselves exempted from any safety guardrails. After all, we should not forget that the Wuhan lab that definitely wasn't definitely was wasn't was may have been the origin of Covid was also co-funded by the US government, and that such "insider" threats are the norm for how biosecurity disasters happen.

So how should we proceed more safely?

In my posts on d/acc ([1] [2]), I've argued that "offense/defense balance" is not an immutable background fact about the world, it's a variable that we can affect in our favor. If we accelerate defensive technologies, then the world becomes not only safer in general, but also safer for pluralism. If we don't have to worry about cyberattacks thanks to formal verification, then we can survive without a lot of restrictive regulation and surveillance of the internet, and we can have much more egalitarian access to tools that may have some offensive uses but are ultimately necessary for modern prosperity.

d/acc in one picture

In the "present-day-style LLMs are only the next industrial revolution" world, this is sufficient. We should really be putting 1% of GDP toward all of this, and that should be the main part of our response. A mission to unite humanity: making the world safe for pluralism.

In the "super-intelligent AI in three years" world, this is not enough. Offense can be done quickly, by anyone anywhere. Defense, on the other hand, is inherently a whole-of-society task: you have to actually get people to upgrade to properly-secure software, walk through hundreds of thousands of pieces of legacy code and ferret out all the bugs, teach your parents to ask security questions, install air filtering and UVC in all the public spaces to slow down the super-viruses, and make sure you can actually mass-produce mRNA-targeted prophylactics on-demand in every country.

Then yes, we need policy tools to force AI progress to slow down, especially forms of progress that are uniquely dangerous. This is why I supported China's moves to push AI labs to remove offensive-cybersecurity-related data from their training processes, I am ok with anti-data-center movements, and in the future I am sure even stronger measures will be required. But there is a right way and a wrong way to do these things.

Let me quote Scott once again, from his post "Be Nice At Least Until You Can Coordinate Meanness":

Society punishes people for crimes, including the crime of libel. Punishment is naturally not-nice, but this seems fair; we can't just have people libeling each other all the time with no consequences. But what makes this tolerable is that it's coordinated – done through the court system according to carefully codified libel law that explains to everybody what is and isn't okay. Remove the coordination aspect, and you've got the old system where if you say something that offends my honor then I get some friends and try to beat you up in a dark alley. The impulse is the same: deploy not-niceness in the worthy goal of preventing libel. But one method is coordinated and the other isn't.

My post "The Most Important Scarce Resource is Legitimacy" was strongly inspired by these ideas.

If we have to do mean things (such as stopping people from making ultra-powerful AIs with offensive-biotech capability, or limiting how many GPUs people have), the right way to do it is a wide-scale broad agreement that has a lot of legitimacy. Only engaging in "coordinated meanness" is an "asymmetric weapon", a weapon stronger in the hands of the good guys than the bad guys. As Scott writes:

As a Jew, if I heard that skinheads were beating up Jews in dark alleys, I would be pretty freaked out; for all I know I could be the next victim. But if I heard that skinheads were circulating a petition to get Congress to expel all the Jews, I wouldn't be freaked out at all. I would expect almost nobody to sign the petition.

If your goal truly is to keep people safe, you should at the least be able to get Carney's Middle Powers, and a whole bunch of other countries that are not US, China or Carney-aligned, and various intellectuals that are not tied to any particular country, onboard. You won't get perfect consensus - the self-interested, e/accs and libertarians more purist than I am will still disagree - but you can get broad support. And if your goal is to dominate, then you will fail at this step - which is the point.

In my view, if we are still alive in thirty years, it will be for one of three reasons:

  • It turned out that "2020s-era AI is ‘only' as powerful as the industrial revolution" was broadly correct
  • AI alignment is easy / default, and in the meantime we accelerate defense successfully
  • We get proper international pressure for a slowdown

I don't see "humanity's fate hinges on whether AI is made by The Good Company or The Bad Company" or "humanity's fate hinges on having 6 more months to do alignment research" as having high probability mass. Hence, to me, preserving optionality for Plan C is worth little.

For this reason, I think SURA ("Stop Unilaterally, Raise Awareness") is a totally fine thing for an individual or organization to do. It's not the optimal thing, but it's a very moral and reasonable thing.

There are two other flavors of SU____ also worth considering:

SUSLUG ("Stop Unilaterally, Start Laboring on Unambiguous Good")

Instead of working on accelerating AI, switch to working on something that may well be 10x less impactful, but is at least an unambiguous good. To me the most natural categories are:

  • Accelerating the d/acc roadmap: formally verifying open-source software and hardware, promoting clean indoor air, figuring out AI-proof social media and democracy, building the global pipeline to be able to give everyone prophylactics that eliminate a super-virus within 4 weeks of first sequencing it, etc.
  • The stuff that we agreed is good in 2019: curing diseases and aging, relieving extreme poverty, etc.

SUDACAR ("Stop Unilaterally, Don't Apologize, Chill And Retire")

For many people, flipping all the way and becoming opponents of the thing they worked on is too much to ask. It's a huge cost, and it requires being very confident that your new position is the right one.

SUDACAR is the "incentive-compatible" alternative. If you have existing wealth from AI, you really can just either go live someplace cheap and find some hobby, or switch away from frontier development to a career that optimizes for enjoying your life. This does not require burning any bridges. For this reason, it's a very available choice simply if you're no longer confident that your work on AI is net-positive for humanity.

This is the ego-deflating approach: do the right thing not by making yourself bigger, but by making yourself smaller.

Retvrn to large-coalition mentality

The above are all small improvements to individual behavior; they don't get us closer to a comprehensive solution. For that, the patch will likely be not individual and technical, but rather social.

In my post on galaxy-brain resistance last year, I ended with a section emphasizing the importance of "having the right social bags", and finished with the advice: "Don't live in the San Francisco Bay Area".

I remember a lot of people who eagerly retweeted the post quietly ignored that line. But I want to revisit it.

The shift toward small-coalition mentality did not happen because a bunch of intellectuals came together and said "let's have a small-coalition mentality now". Well okay, a few intellectuals did, but it's hardly the majority or what I would consider respectable people. Rather, I suspect that the shift toward small-coalition mentality happened because of subconscious pressures that arose naturally because of the shift from a community with shared interests but no shared social circles or shared incentives, toward a smaller and more established community that shared all three.

The start of the solution is to find, and start executing on, any and all strategies to reverse this.

First, here is one crazy strawman idea for an AI lab:

  • Set up a totally separately governed and owned entity in Canada, with totally separate shareholders and operators.
  • Set up the primary offices of the two entities in Seattle and Vancouver (London and Paris could also work)
  • Do training of all future frontier models in a format where the bottom 1/3 of layers are kept and trained on one side, and the top 2/3 are kept and trained on the other side. The full weights are never coalesced in one single place. (minimum round-trip latency between Seattle and Vancouver is 4ms, so this is viable: you would be capped at 250 tok/s of inference, maybe 100 with overhead, which is faster than most frontier model inference today)

If desired, you can use trusted hardware as a second layer of defense, so it's hard for either side to even do anything with the weights they hold without the other's consent (and even add third parties into the authority structure, maybe even run the authorization mechanism on the blockchain). I would not trust trusted hardware as the only layer of defense, but it's worth adding on top.

Basically, if everyone can't have the weights, no one should have the weights, and verifiably so. Additionally, at least two separate entities in two separate jurisdictions, ideally more, should each have a unilateral kill switch.

This seems like a much more large-coalition-friendly and broadly palatable "build AI inside a box" plan than one company racing ahead.

It addition to its technical properties, it also creates a social and political game that forces that AI lab to be very-large-coalition from that moment forward.

But we should not overly convince ourselves that the primary heroes here will be AI builders. The more important heroes may well be coalition-builders: storytellers (including social media memelords), lawyers, international diplomats, as well as builders of technologies that make verifiable AI safety possible (and open defensive tech that makes the world safe for pluralism). The most important thing here is not any specific technical plan; rather, it's the fact that we need to urgently retvrn to strategies that bear a much more large-coalition mindset. And until that happens, yes, it's okay to SU____.
