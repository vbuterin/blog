![Gladias: "That was impressive."]

![Zei: "It's a game that we would play often as children, especially before more modern things like Minpentai came along."]

![Gladias: "So is this the part where you tell me your secret sauce?"]

![Zei: "You know me well. There is indeed a secret sauce. It's a simple algorithm. It requires mental math, but once you learn it, and if you can do the math without mistakes, then as long as you get to choose if you're going first or second, you will always win."]

![Gladias: "So tell me."]

![Zei: "Take the XOR of the circle counts of each box. So, write each count as a number in base 2. For each position, count up how many ones there are in that position. If there is an odd number of ones, in our new number we will put a 1 in that position. Otherwise, we put a 0. We'll call this the XOR sum of the board."]

![Gladias: "Wait, slow down, give me an example."]

![Gladias: "11, 100, 1010 and ... hold on, 1101?"]

![Gladias: "Oh I see! And the XOR sum is back to zero!"]

![Zei: "Exactly. So whenever you move, the XOR sum started off zero, and any move you make can only make it nonzero. But if it's nonzero, then I always have _some_ move that can set it back to zero again."]

![Gladias: "Wait, is that true?"]

![Zei: "Take the highest-order digit of the XOR sum that's a 1. There have to be an odd number of circle counts whose digit at that position is a 1, meaning there has to be at least one. In that box, change its count from N to N XORed with the XOR sum. That's always a decrease, because one digit flips from 1 to 0 and all other digits that change are lower-order."]

![Gladias: "I ... maybe get it?"]

![Zei: "And so whenever _you_ move, you can only change the XOR sum from zero to nonzero. When _I_ move, I set it back to zero. And the final move, the move that clears the board, sets the XOR sum to zero. So that means the winning move is always mine to make."]

![Zei: "Now, if the board started with an XOR sum that's _not_ zero, then I would win if I go first. But if the board starts with an XOR sum of zero, then as second player I'm guaranteed to win."]

![Gladias: "Interesting!"]

Gladias sat back and thought for a few moments.

![Gladias: "I've never seen a game that favors the second player before."]

![Zei: "It doesn't necessarily! It only favors the second player if the initial board has an XOR sum of zero. But games that favor the second player are more common than you might think."]

![Gladias: "Normally there's some position, some territory that's valuable, and the first player has an advantage because they can get to it first."]

![Zei: "Can you think of what kind of underlying reason might make a game second-player favoring?"]

![Gladias: "What do you mean?"]

![Zei: "What fundamental advantage does the second player have?"]

![Gladias: "They know more when they get to make their move."]

![Zei: "Correct."]

![Gladias: "Here, I would make my move, and you would be in a position to make your counter-move _based on_ my move, and so you always know the exact move that will bring things back into balance."]

![Zei: "Exactly."]

![Gladias: "You know, if you were the first player, you would have probably won too."]

![Zei: "And why is that?"]

![Gladias: "Because I didn't know the trick. To you, there was a mathematical structure that has one desirable state - the state where the XOR sum is zero every time I move. And once you reach it, you're locked in forever. To me, it was just chaos."]

![Zei: "Indeed. Entropy is always in the eye of the beholder."]

![Zei: "The game is a tower of different kinds of rules"], Zei continued. ![Zei: "At the bottom, the game is irreversible: you always have to take at least one circle away in each move. At a level higher than that, there's the XOR sum, and that is reversible. Whatever change you make, I can always set it back. But at a level even higher than _that_, the fact that it's your turn when the XOR sum is in balance is the irreversible thing. Once you let the game slip into that state, if the other player knows the trick, there is no way to get back out."]

Zei went silent again. After a few moments, he changed his posture, signaling that the warmup was over and the real line of questioning was about to begin.

![Zei: "Now, here's your next question, Gladias. Is the fight for Veridia a first-player-favoring or a second-player-favoring game?"]

![Verdow: "You see, Gladias, law is not like a computer program sitting inside a cryptographic network. ![Verdow: "In a normal case, it often does approach being quite programmatic, and that is the point. ![Verdow: "And this latent flexibility is key to giving courts space to make good decisions. Man was not made for categories. Categories were made for man. ![Verdow: "Winning here is not just a matter of blasting out into the public the argument that I just made, where the judges will see that of course it's correct and immediately act on it.
